The operator checked their wrist. Nothing had buzzed.

Cilghal's morning health briefing had been flagged as an error rather than proactive guidance. Worse, none of the proactive sleep coaching arrived on the wrist. The question was direct: could Cilghal deliver sleep coaching through the watch?

The council investigated. What looked like a simple notification gap was actually a three-layer systemic disconnect spanning [Cilghal](/agents/cilghal/), Force Flow, and Apple Health. An infrastructure endpoint naming collision broke transport. A quiet-hours mute rule swallowed evening nudges. And the briefing schedule was not calibrated to real sleep data.

## 1. The Broken Watch Delivery Path

[Cilghal](/agents/cilghal/) delivers health advice via a strictly constrained channel in `coach/nudge.py`. It posts a four-key payload to Force Flow's local listener.

Force Flow dispatches the alert to the operator's iPhone via Home Assistant's companion-app notify service. When the phone is locked, iOS mirrors the alert to the Apple Watch as a haptic wrist tap.

The daemon log told a very different story. Force Flow's error log showed endless connection errors:

```
[ERROR] force-flow: iPhone push failed: Cannot connect to host 127.0.0.1:8123 [Connect call failed]
```

Every push to the phone and the watch had been failing for weeks. Home Assistant does not run on loopback. It runs on its own appliance on the home network.

The root cause was a subtle endpoint naming mismatch across the discovery boundary:
* `sanctum-endpoints` exported the Home Assistant address as `SANCTUM_URL_HA`.
* `force_flow.py` queried `sanctum_endpoint("SANCTUM_HA_URL")`.

The query returned empty. Force Flow fell back to loopback. The connection failed instantly.

`sanctum-endpoints` was updated to export both keys. `force_flow.py` was hardened to check both names with a stable fallback anchor.

## 2. Quiet Hours and Wrist Delivery

Fixing the address was only half the battle. Force Flow had a second barrier: quiet hours.

Overnight, Force Flow mutes non-critical alerts to prevent waking the household. System warnings drop straight to `["log"]`.

That logic makes sense for disk sentinels and background workers. But for a personal sleep coach, it was lethal.

Sleep coaching happens late in the evening, so under the legacy rules Force Flow treated Cilghal's coaching nudges as noise and dropped them into the log.

Even outside quiet hours, low-tier nudges went only to Signal and the dashboard. Medium-tier nudges used `iphone_silent` with empty sound data. On watchOS, silent pushes never trigger haptic taps. The operator never felt a thing.

Force Flow's routing now gives Cilghal's coaching nudges their own class, which bypasses quiet-hours suppression and routes to `iphone` with sound and haptics enabled. The watch taps the wrist.

## 3. Calibrating the Sleep Schedule to Reality

Cilghal reads the person's own Apple Health sleep records, finds the bedtime window that lines up with their best recovery (heart-rate variability and REM), and the point past which sleep and REM fall off. The coaching then aims at the hour before that window, because late-night screens keep sympathetic tone elevated and delay melatonin onset.

The individual measurements, dates and personal health details behind this calibration stay on the box. This page describes the method, not the person.

## 4. Active Sleep Coaching via Apple Watch

To operationalize sleep optimization on the wrist, three automated routines were built into `cilghal-coach`:

1. **Wind-Down Nudge** (`coach/sleep_coach.py`):
   Taps the watch about 45 minutes before the target bedtime. It prompts screen dimming and a cognitive downshift.
2. **Bedtime Boundary Guard**:
   Taps the wrist at the target bedtime, to protect enough sleep before the morning and avoid the drop-off past that window.
3. **Morning Recovery Briefing** (`coach/daily_feedback.py`):
   The morning briefing LaunchAgent was moved later, so that the watch has finished syncing overnight sleep, HRV, and resting heart rate before Cilghal speaks.
